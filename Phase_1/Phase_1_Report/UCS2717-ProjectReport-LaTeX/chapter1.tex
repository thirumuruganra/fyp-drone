Social media platforms have been identified as the main channel of digital communication, but because of their open and unstructured nature, they are highly vulnerable to social engineering attacks like phishing, baiting, pretexting, whaling, and malicious URL attacks. These types of attacks use linguistic tricks, deceptive URLs, and behavioral patterns to target the security of users. The reason why it is difficult to detect these attacks is that the discriminative information is distributed across multiple heterogeneous channels like text semantics, URL graph properties. Moreover, the attack patterns keep on varying, leading to concept drift and catastrophic forgetting in the conventional static learning models.

To overcome these issues, the proposed system brings forth a multi-phase detection framework with a structured architecture based on the text and URL information of the emails. The preprocessing phase of the system is responsible for the normalization, lemmatization, URL segmentation, and statistical standardization of the feature representation. The feature extraction phase is carried out using a two-pipeline approach. The first pipeline consists of TF-IDF vectorization and Truncated SVD, followed by the application of a Stacked Autoencoder (SAE) for the extraction of a 100-dimensional deep latent feature representation through unsupervised reconstruction learning.

The second pipeline provides a Fuzzy Probability-Guided Feature Descriptor Generator (Fuzzy-PGFDG) to capture the uncertainty and boundary effects that are typical of social engineering data. Based on the manually designed linguistic and URL feature descriptors, five fuzzy membership values are calculated: Typicality, Density, Homogeneity, Outlier Score, and Fuzzy Entropy, which capture the degree of belongingness to the classes in a probabilistic environment. The final feature vectors from the deep SAE-based pipeline and the fuzzy descriptor pipeline are combined to create a hybrid feature vector of dimension 124, which allows for complementary learning in the structured, statistical, and uncertainty domains.

To facilitate adaptability in the presence of dynamically changing data streams, the framework incorporates a Data Augmented Incremental Learning (DAIL) paradigm that retains knowledge of previous classes using representative samples instead of retaining the raw data. This approach helps to alleviate the problem of catastrophic forgetting while ensuring memory efficiency and scalability. Classification is carried out by an Extreme Learning Machine (ELM) with hyperparameter tuning for different hidden neuron settings and activation functions. Model selection is driven by a gap-penalized criterion that balances empirical risk minimization and generalization strength. The ELM is trained incrementally by replacing the existing models. The combination of deep representation learning, fuzzy probabilistic modeling, incremental learning, and optimized classification provides a scalable and robust framework for dynamic social engineering attack detection.


\section{MOTIVATION}
The growing frequency of social media attacks on social media platforms has created an urgent demand for clever, automated detection solutions. Social engineering attacks exploit users' trust by using malicious URLs, false email subject lines, and manipulative message content. These assaults are far more difficult to detect since they target users' human psychology rather than the system's technical characteristics. This is why automated and adaptive detection is a key need in today's cyber security.

The dynamic and unstructured nature of social media and email messages is one of the primary motivations for pursuing this topic. An attack can include multiple signs at the same time, including password-stealing phrases in everyday communication, urgent messages to press the user, and an attack URL embedded in the message. A system that can simultaneously assess the message content and URL structure information is necessary for identifying such assaults.

The existing machine learning solutions have been constrained by their reliance
on manually designed features, which are time-consuming to design and do not effectively represent the deeply hidden and nonlinear patterns that exist in complex email and URL data. This drawback led to the incorporation of a Stacked Autoencoder(SAE) to automatically discover meaningful and compact
representations from the raw input data through unsupervised reconstruction, without the need for manual feature selection.

Although the deep features obtained from the autoencoder are capable of
representing the structural and statistical patterns, they do not represent the
uncertainty or class membership of the ambiguous samples that are located near
the decision boundaries. This drawback led to the incorporation of a Fuzzy
Probability-Guided Feature Descriptor Generator (Fuzzy-PGFDG) to enhance the
feature representation with meaningful fuzzy membership values such as
Typicality, Density, Homogeneity, Outlier Score, and Fuzzy Entropy.

The evolution of attack patterns over time is another, and no less significant, explanation. Attacks using social engineering are constantly evolving, with new tactics and types of attacks always emerging. The ability of an attack detection model to identify new, undiscovered attack types will soon be rendered obsolete if it was trained and fixed on a static dataset. Retraining the model from scratch each time fresh data is received is computationally impractical and renders all previously acquired knowledge unusable. To learn incrementally, DAIL (Data Augmented Incremental Learning) was used to update the model with new attack data. Additionally, a column-alternating augmentation technique was used to retain knowledge of previously seen classes by creating artificial data samples that represented attack data from previous classes without storing it.

Finally, the reason for choosing ELM as the classifier was its efficiency in computational calculation, analytical calculation of weights, and its outstanding generalization performance when dealing with high-quality hybrid features. The ability to be applied in situations where rapid updates of the model are needed with every new batch of data makes it a perfect fit for the proposed system’s application.

\section{BACKGROUND}
With the growing popularity of social media and online communication platforms, the trend of social platform attacks has intensified. These platforms are used by social engineers to attack a large number of users at once by sending misleading text messages, inserting malicious URLs, and impersonating trusted sources. The issue of detecting harmful actions has remained difficult due to the unstructured, diversified, and continuously changing nature of social media information. Conventional detection techniques, such as blacklisting, rule-based filtering, and keyword matching systems, are inadequate as attackers often change their behavior patterns frequently, making it easy for them to evade detection by changing the wording, URL format, or source address.

To make detection more accurate and robust, there has been a gradual movement in the research community toward the use of automated computational models that can process multiple data sources together. Early machine learning models relied heavily on manual feature engineering, where experts in the domain designed features manually. This is a time-consuming, domain-specific, and inadequate approach to feature engineering, as it is not capable of extracting the underlying, deeply hidden non-linear patterns that exist in the URL structure and natural language text content.

To address this drawback, deep learning-based feature extraction has been employed extensively. Among these, the Stacked Autoencoder (SAE) has been shown to be very successful. A Stacked Autoencoder is a dimensionality reduction technique that reduces high-dimensional input data into a compact, lower-dimensional encoded form by learning to reconstruct the input data using a bottleneck architecture of stacked encoder and decoder layers. For this project, individual autoencoders are trained for text features (reducing a 200-dimensional TF-IDF SVD feature to 50 dimensions) and URL features (reducing a 68-dimensional feature to 50 dimensions), resulting in a combined 100-dimensional deep feature vector. Importantly, the autoencoder is trained only on Day 1 data and its weights are locked for all subsequent incremental days, providing a stable feature space for the entire learning process.

Though autoencoders perform admirably on learning structural and distributional patterns, they are deterministic in nature and fail to capture the gradual and uncertain nature of class boundaries, which is a major drawback in distinguishing semantically similar attack categories like phishing and pretexting attacks. To overcome this, the Fuzzy-PGFDG pipeline has been introduced, which uses fuzzy logic-based feature generation. The concept of fuzzy sets, first proposed by Zadeh, allows the representation of partial membership in classes, as opposed to binary membership.Based on membership functions that are defined by the estimates of Mahalanobis distance, neighborhood density, and entropy, five fuzzy features: typicality, density, homogeneity, outlier score, and fuzzy entropy are derived for each sample in this setup. Once these features are incorporated into the manually designed 19-dimensional feature space, a 24-dimensional fuzzy-hybrid feature vector is formed. This feature vector is then concatenated with the output of the autoencoder to form the final 124-dimensional feature vector.

The assumption of static data distributions, in which the models are trained just once and are not modified, is one of the most important missing assumptions in the detection systems in use today. In actuality, new attack patterns are created throughout time, and social engineering assault patterns are always changing. This necessitates the use of an incremental 7 learning strategy, which allows the detection model to be updated with each new batch of data while retaining the knowledge gained from earlier batches.In order to solve this problem, Madhusudhanan and Jaganathan's DAIL (Data Augmented Incremental Learning) architecture uses column-alternating data augmentation, a method that creates new data by switching the values of the feature columns of pairs of pre-existing samples. This prevents catastrophic forgetting while being memory-efficient since it guarantees that the new samples are always in the same feature space as their parent cluster, enabling the model to retain its prior class knowledge without having to store the actual data.

As an incremental learning classifier for classification, the Extreme Learning Machine (ELM) is used. Unlike conventional backpropagation-based networks, ELM is a single-hidden-layer feedforward network that trains much more quickly since the input weights are randomly allocated and the output weights are calculated analytically using the Moore-Penrose pseudoinverse. In this project, an automated hyperparameter search is used to test different hidden layer sizes and activation functions. The best combination is chosen based on a gap-penalized scoring metric that prioritizes test accuracy over large train-test splits across all four days of incremental learning.

\section{PROBLEM DEFINITION}
Massive volumes of unstructured and diverse data are generated every second by social media platforms, which makes them an ideal target for malicious URL distribution, phishing, baiting, pretexting, and whaling attacks. Conventional rule- and signature-based security systems struggle to identify these kinds of assaults since they rely on human trust and behavior patterns. A learning model based on historical data becomes obsolete very quickly since the patterns of these attacks are not static; new patterns and attack types are constantly emerging.

The data's high dimensionality and multimodality present the first technical difficulty. Two distinct modalities—the language and structure of the URL and the email text—are used to characterize the attack elements. Preprocessing pipelines and feature extraction techniques vary per modality. The modalities' unprocessed features are redundant, noisy, and high-dimensional. This necessitates the use of efficient representation learning methods capable of converting raw features into a low-dimensional representation that is noise-free and retains discriminative information.

Due to the combined need to adjust to changing attack patterns, different kinds of data, and the shortcomings of traditional models, a system must be able to execute incremental learning in addition to deep feature learning and fuzzy-based feature extraction. All three needs cannot be met at the same time by the current methods since they rely on feature set knowledge, ignore the ambiguity of class boundaries, or necessitate retraining the entire system if new attack classes emerge. Through the integration of a DAIL-based incremental learning framework with column-alternating augmentation, a Fuzzy-PGFDG for interpretable membership function modeling, and a Stacked Autoencoder for deep feature learning, the suggested system simultaneously satisfies all three objectives.
