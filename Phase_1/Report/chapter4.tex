\chapter{EXPERIMENTAL RESULTS AND PERFORMANCE ANALYSIS}\label{cha:results}

This chapter describes the data the system runs on, the hardware and software used, the
experiments carried out so far and what their results show. The numbers come from the
first pass of the evaluation cycle in Section~\ref{sec:m5}, from a controlled wind
experiment, from the ablation runner and from the first SITL flights. They are
preliminary in a specific sense: MOPSO was run at reduced settings, the risk weights and
battery constants are not yet calibrated, and the synthetic density labels have a known
defect. We state each caveat next to the result it affects, so that the reader can judge
how far the result can be pushed.

\section{DATASET DESCRIPTION}\label{sec:dataset}

Route planning papers rarely publish their environments. The datasets of the works we
reviewed are custom, proprietary or not released, so a direct numerical comparison on
their data is not possible. As is common in this field, we built our own environments.
There are two kinds: synthetic airspace graphs, generated from fixed seeds, for
controlled benchmarks; and a real-region lattice built from public geographic and
weather data. Table~\ref{tab:datasets} lists every graph used in this chapter.

\subsection{Synthetic airspace graphs}

The synthetic graphs come from the seeded builder of Section~\ref{sec:m1}. Each is a
jittered grid of about 1.3\,km spacing with 8-neighbour directed edges, seeded Gaussian
hills for terrain, obstacle discs, and seeded wind between 12 and 28\,km/h with a gust
factor of 1.4. The three scales (50, 200 and 500 nodes) were chosen to cover a sanity
check, a mid-sized core case and a stress case. Because every draw comes from the seed,
anyone can regenerate the exact graph from the seed and the code.

There is one known defect in this data. The obstacle density labels (5\%, 20\% and
40\%) do not reach the graph builder. The benchmark runner scales the final risk instead,
so the three scenarios differ in size and seed, but not in obstacle coverage as the
labels suggest. Fixing this is part of the next pass, and every benchmark number will be
recomputed after it.

\subsection{Real-region lattice}

The real-region lattice is built from four public data sources, listed in
Table~\ref{tab:sources}. The method is region-agnostic; we use one coastal urban
neighbourhood as the example region. The default 6\,km box at 200\,m spacing gives 900
candidate nodes, of which 893 remain after node selection and connectivity pruning in the fixture run, linked by
6,752 directed edges.

Public Overpass servers are not always reliable. Live fetches sometimes reset or time
out, and a failed fetch falls back to empty layers while the run still completes. For
repeatable offline runs the project therefore includes fixture layers, hand-made test
data shaped like the real region. The ablation in Section~\ref{sec:perf-ablation} used
these fixtures. They show how each layer acts on the planner, but they are not
real-world measurements, and the live-data run is part of the next pass.

\subsection{Payload tag space and simulation bundle}

Tier~2 is evaluated over all 225 tag combinations, with four reference presets
(Table~\ref{tab:presets}) that stand for risk-dominated, distance-dominated,
energy-dominated and balanced deliveries. For simulation, a 1.5\,km region is frozen
into a bundle whose hash ties the planner graph, the stored front, the mission file and
the Gazebo world together.

\section{ECOSYSTEM}\label{sec:ecosystem}

\subsection{Hardware configuration}

All planner experiments in this chapter ran on a laptop-class CPU without a GPU. The
planner does not need one: Tier~1 is a graph search that runs on a single core, and
Tier~2 is a closed-form computation. The simulation runs under WSL2 (Ubuntu 24.04) on
the same class of machine.

Phase~II moves the system onto standard, commercially available hardware: an F450
quadcopter frame with a Pixhawk flight controller running ArduCopter, a Raspberry Pi~5
companion computer that hosts Tier~2 on board, a servo payload release and a MAVLink
telemetry link to the ground station.

\subsection{Software environment}

All code is written in Python~3. NetworkX~\cite{r5} holds the directed airspace
graph and runs the A* baselines; NumPy handles vectorised dominance checks and metrics;
Shapely~2 STRtree indexes hold buildings, zones, water and population classes;
GeoPandas, pyogrio and OSMnx~\cite{r4} parse and cache the OpenStreetMap layers; and the
requests library calls Open-Meteo, OpenTopography and Overpass. Figures are drawn with
Matplotlib, the operator dashboard uses Tkinter, and the test suites use unittest and
run offline without keys. Simulation uses Gazebo Harmonic~\cite{r24}, ArduPilot
SITL~\cite{r23}, pymavlink over MAVLink~\cite{r7} and QGroundControl. Diagrams were
drawn in PlantUML and the report typeset in \LaTeX.

The settings were as follows. In E1, all methods used the same source and target per
scenario, and HV, IGD and spread were computed against the union reference front. MOPSO
ran at a reduced setting of 24 particles, 30 iterations and 2 repetitions per scenario
to get a complete first pass quickly; the report-scale run (100, 200 and 30) is the next
step. In E2, the S2 graph (196 nodes, 1,404 edges, seed 202) was costed under calm air
and under a 25\,km/h wind with 35\,km/h gusts from each of eight compass directions, then
in 15$^\circ$ steps, with the graph, layers, source and target unchanged. E3 used a flat
3\,km lattice with no terrain, buildings or zones under a 25\,km/h east wind. E4 rebuilt
the real-region front with all layers, without wind, without terrain and without

front (16 routes), the S3 front (115 routes) and a 2\,km lattice. E6 flew planner
missions from a fixture bundle against ArduCopter SITL (4.8.0-dev, built-in quadcopter
physics, five times real time), and a Tier~2 mission was also flown in the Gazebo city
world. E7 covers about 220 planner tests and 45 simulation tests, all offline;
Appendix~\ref{app:tests} lists them. One of them compares the NAMOA* front with a
brute-force front over all simple paths on a small graph, which is our strongest check
that Tier~1 is exact.

NAMOA* has an IGD of zero in every scenario, so its front contains every point of the
reference front. That is what an exact method should give, and it confirms that the
pruning rules do not throw away non-dominated routes. MOPSO recovers 12 of the 16 routes
on S2 and 38 of the 115 on S3, and its HV is close to that of NAMOA*, with a gap of
0.0004 on S2 and 0.009 on S3.

The HV gap is small because MOPSO's archive still reaches the extremes of the front
while missing most interior points, and interior points are the ones Tier~2 needs for
mixed payloads. For this project the IGD and front-size columns say more than HV. NAMOA* also spreads its points more
evenly, with $\Delta$ of 0.666 against 0.814 on S3. On S1 both methods find the same
two-route front, which tells us the smallest scenario is too easy to separate them.

The single-objective A* routes reach a high HV only when their one objective happens to
dominate the volume, as A* energy does on S2. None of them can cover the front, and their
IGD stays between 0.44 and 1.12. Figure~\ref{fig:bench-hv} plots HV per method.

\begin{figure}[!htbp]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=0.62\textwidth]{images/benchmark_hv.png}
\caption{Hypervolume per method and scenario (first pass)}\label{fig:bench-hv}
\end{figure}

Exactness has a price. On S3, NAMOA* took 33.8\,s against 4.3\,s for MOPSO, , and the
runtime grows quickly with front size. Tier~1 runs once
per region and weather window, not once per mission, so half a minute is acceptable,
but it is still the main performance target for the next pass. Options include front
thinning, a tighter heuristic and a coarser lattice over open areas.



On S2 the HV values of 1.2065 and 1.2061 are too close to claim superiority without
the 30 seeded MOPSO repetitions and a statistical test, which the next pass will run.
The IGD result does not have this problem, since an IGD of zero cannot be beaten.

The first observation is that direction alone matters a great deal. At the same speed,
the cheapest route on the front needs 24.58 energy units with the wind from the
south-west and 31.34 with it from the north-east, a difference of 27.5\%. A planner that
used wind speed without direction would assign both cases the same cost.

Risk and energy also respond to different properties of the wind. Opposite directions
(0$^\circ$ and 180$^\circ$, or 45$^\circ$ and 225$^\circ$) give the same minimum risk,
because crosswind depends only on the axis of the wind. They give very different minimum
energy, because the headwind penalty depends on which way along that axis the drone is
flying. This is what Equation~(\ref{eq:hwcw}) predicts, and it is why the model
needs both components.

Wind also changes the shape of the front. The front shrinks from 36 routes in calm air
to between 6 and 14, and the four presets land on only two distinct routes. Under a
steady wind, edges with strong crosswind are both costlier and riskier, so energy and
risk become correlated and fewer real trade-offs are left. The fragile medical pick is
the lowest-risk option on every windy front, but its energy varies from 26.87 to 33.63
with direction. Figure~\ref{fig:wind-sweep} repeats the experiment in 15$^\circ$ steps.
The minimum energy moves between 24.6 and 31.6 units, a 28.5\% swing, and the front holds
between 6 and 15 routes.

\begin{figure}[!htbp]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{images/wind_direction_sweep.png}
\caption{Front size, minimum energy and minimum risk on S2 as a 25\,km/h wind turns
through 360$^\circ$; the dashed line is calm air}\label{fig:wind-sweep}
\end{figure}

The collapse from four distinct picks to two may be the right answer for a front with
fewer real trade-offs, or a side effect of min-max normalisation on a short front. The
next pass will test both, and the answer may change Tier~2.

On the 115-route S3 front, the four presets choose four different routes, and three of
them are non-extreme points. Disaster relief takes the minimum-energy route, which

The emergency medicine pick deserves a closer look. It is only the 22nd-shortest route,
yet it is just 1.9\% longer than the shortest route on the front. Its weights are
dominated by distance, but not entirely, so the utility accepts a very small distance
penalty for a lower risk. A single-objective planner cannot make that trade, and a
fixed-weight planner would make the same trade for every payload.

The fragile medical and emergency picks show the size of the trade-off Tier~2 is
choosing between. The fragile route is 10.5\% longer than the emergency route (41.02
against 37.11) and carries 71\% less cumulative risk (1.92 against 6.56). The fragile
pick is 25th of 115 on risk rather than first, because its distance and energy weights
are not zero, and the routes with even lower risk cost much more distance. Mixed cargo
lands between the two on every objective, which is what its balanced weights ask for.
Every selection took about 0.14\,ms on S3, thousands of times inside the one-second
target.

On the smaller S2 front the picks are less spread out. Emergency medicine and disaster
relief share the minimum-energy route, which on this graph is also nearly the shortest.
This is the same pattern as in the wind experiment, and it points to front size as the
factor that limits how many distinct choices Tier~2 can make.

\subsection{Simulation results}

In E6 the SITL mission runner flew planner missions against ArduCopter SITL. Mission
upload, read-back, the switch to AUTO, the landing and the disarm all succeeded. The
cross-track root-mean-square error between the flown and planned tracks was
0.11--0.13\,m, and the landing offset from the destination pad was 0.01--0.02\,m. These
numbers show that the mission chain from Tier~2 to the autopilot is correct, including
the altitude frame, which is easy to get wrong over uneven terrain.

Figure~\ref{fig:sitl} shows a Tier~2 mission flown in AUTO inside the Gazebo city world,
with the MAVProxy console on the left and the mission in QGroundControl on the right.
The quantitative results above come from the runs with ArduPilot's built-in physics. The
Gazebo world has no wind yet, and the battery constants are still placeholders, so
logged comparisons across wind cases in Gazebo are planned for the next pass.
